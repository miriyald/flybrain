Each bar is one output neuron: the approach readout minus the avoid readout, summed over whichever Kenyon cells are firing right now. Both readouts only ever shrink, so the raw numbers are usually negative and only their order means anything — the bars are scaled between the worst and best on screen, so the fullest one is always the action taken. Mid-air they hold still, because the arc is committed and there is nothing to decide.

Kenyon cells firing

All 1,927 of them. About one cell in twenty is lit at any moment — that sparseness is the whole trick. Watch the pattern turn over as an obstacle closes in.

How it learned. There is no label here, only a crash — and the crash arrives about thirty frames after the decision that caused it. So the synapse holds a decaying tag of what fired recently, and when dopamine finally arrives it acts on the tag. Clearing an obstacle is reward and weakens the avoidance of what was just done; crashing is punishment and weakens the approach to it. Both act only by weakening — nothing here is ever strengthened, and no error signal travels backwards.

Credit where due. The game, its constants and its sprite sheet are ported from santifiorino/dino-reinforcement-learning, a Processing sketch that evolves a population of a thousand players with a genetic algorithm — every hitbox, the jump parabola and the cactus and bird tables here were read from its source. The artwork is originally Chromium's offline dinosaur game. What runs on this page is not that algorithm: there is no population and no evolution, just one circuit learning inside a single lifetime.
