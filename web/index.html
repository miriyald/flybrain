How a smell circuit reads a number

The mushroom body is where a fly stores what it has learned about odours. Its architecture, though, has nothing chemical about it — it is a general way of turning a crowded input into a tag that is easy to learn from. So it will take pixels just as readily.

The expansion is the important part. Two inputs that arrive looking similar land on almost separate handfuls of Kenyon cells, so teaching the circuit one digit barely disturbs what it knows about the others. Computer science found the same trick independently and calls it locality-sensitive hashing.

Learning by forgetting

Nothing here was trained by backpropagation. Dopamine in a fly only ever weakens synapses, so the circuit is taught by spoiling every wrong answer rather than reinforcing the right one — and a single pass over the training digits works better than repeating them. The whole rule is one line: weights[fired, wrong] *= 0.75.

The fly has two dopamine systems and both are used here: one teaches the outputs that pull toward a choice, the other those that push away. Using only half the circuit costs about eight points of accuracy.

The wiring is measured biology. The thresholds are not: a connectome records how many synapses join two neurons, never how readily either one fires. That gap is filled by hand here, and it is the honest reason to call this a circuit borrowed from a fly rather than a fly's brain.
